Some review tools ask customers to rate their experience first. Happy customers are sent to Google, and unhappy ones are sent to a private feedback form. It sounds clever: more five-star reviews, fewer bad ones. But this practice has a name, review gating, and it can put your Google profile at risk.

What is review gating?

Review gating is any setup that asks for reviews differently depending on whether the customer is likely to be positive. Common examples include:

  • A "How did we do?" screen where four or five stars go to Google and one to three stars go to a private form.
  • Only sending review requests to customers your team marked as happy.
  • Showing the Google link to unhappy customers, but making it much harder to find than the private feedback option.

What Google says

Google's policies for reviews say businesses shouldn't discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively ask for positive reviews from customers. Reviews that break these rules can be removed. Repeated or serious breaches can lead to restrictions on your Business Profile. In practice, the reviews you worked hard to collect are the first thing at risk.

What Australian law says

Under the Australian Consumer Law, businesses must not mislead consumers. The ACCC has warned that hiding or suppressing negative reviews can give a misleading picture of a business. In 2017, the Federal Court ordered property group Meriton to pay $3 million in penalties after it stopped guests it expected to be unhappy from receiving review invitations. The lesson for small businesses is the same: curating which customers get to review you is risky.

"But my tool still shows a public review button"

Many systems offer a private feedback form to unhappy customers with a smaller "leave a public review" button underneath. That's better than blocking them completely, but if the experience still steers unhappy customers away from Google while happy customers go straight there, it can still be treated as selective. The safest approach is to give every customer the same, equally easy path to leave a public review.

What to do instead

  • Ask everyone the same way. Send every customer the same request with your direct Google review link.
  • Offer feedback, don't redirect. It's fine to invite customers to contact you directly if something went wrong, as long as the review link is just as easy to use.
  • Get the volume up. The best protection against the occasional bad review is a steady flow of genuine good ones. A single one-star review matters far less among 200 reviews than among 10.
  • Reply well. A calm, professional reply to a negative review shows future customers how you handle problems.
